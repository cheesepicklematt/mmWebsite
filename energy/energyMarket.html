The ladder

Every five minutes, Australia’s National Electricity Market runs an auction and lands on one number per region. That number can be −$1,000 or $23,200. This page is an attempt to build the intuition for why — by letting you run the auction yourself.

Part one

One auction, one price

There is no direct selling of electricity in the NEM. It is a gross pool: every generator offers into one auction, AEMO’s dispatch engine clears it, and everyone settles at the resulting regional price.

Each unit submits a ladder of up to ten price–volume bands: “give me 280 MW once the price reaches $12.98, another 100 MW at $24.57…” AEMO pools every band from every unit, sorts them cheapest-first, and walks up the staircase until the cumulative megawatts meet demand. The band it stops on sets the price for everybody. Not pay-as-bid — a single clearing price.

A bid is not a price you accept. It is a threshold: dispatch me once the price gets here. Everything below the clearing price earns the full clearing price anyway.

Try a scenario

Three things the staircase makes obvious

  1. Almost nobody is paid what they bid. The solar farm offering at −$55 collects the same $/MWh as the gas turbine that set the price. The gap between offer and clearing price is where a generator’s margin lives — and it is why offering low is not the same as earning low.
  2. Price is a step function, not a curve. Nudge demand across a step edge and the price can multiply. Push it past the last cheap block and there is nothing between you and a $14,500 band. Ten per cent more demand can mean ten times the price.
  3. Bidding below zero is rational. A coal unit that shuts down has to pay to restart, and a unit that has sold a hedge still owes on it whether or not it generates. Both make it worth paying, briefly, to stay dispatched. That is what the −$983 band is.

Part two

A day, five minutes at a time

The ladder above is one interval. A day is 288 of them. Demand rises and falls, the sun comes up and goes down, and the same auction re-runs every five minutes against a stack whose cheap end keeps changing size. Press play and watch the price fall out of the mechanism.

That midday collapse and evening spike is the duck curve, and it is not a quirk of the weather — it is arithmetic. Solar adds several thousand megawatts to the bottom of the stack for six hours, pushing the clearing point down into the negative bands. At sunset it withdraws over about ninety minutes, exactly as demand peaks, and the clearing point walks up into gas.

Prices are a commitment; volumes are a response

The asymmetry that makes the whole thing work: price bands are locked in at 12:30pm the day before and cannot change during the trading day. Volume bands can be rebid until about five minutes before dispatch. Across 573 ladders on a recent day, exactly zero changed a price band. Batteries rebid volumes 288 times — every interval. The median thermal unit submitted once and left it.

And the dispatch engine is deliberately fuel-blind. A band is a price and a number of megawatts; nothing tells the engine whether it is coal, wind or a battery. What the bid does carry is physics — ramp rates, minimum load, energy limits. Fuel type lives in a separate registration table, and the merit order sorts itself out.

The last piece is that most participants are hedged. A generator and a retailer who sign a $80/MWh swap both end up at $80 regardless of what the pool does — the contract settles in cash and the electricity never notices. AEMO has no idea the contract exists. But the contract changes how you bid: fully contracted, you must generate, so you bid the floor. Uncontracted, you would rather keep the spike, so you withhold into high bands. The derivatives market has zero mechanical effect on dispatch and is the single largest influence on it.
